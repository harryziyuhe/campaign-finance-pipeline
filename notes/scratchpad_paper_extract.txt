Buying Access or Buying Elections?
Sectoral Partisan Alignment and Corporate PAC Contributions
under Electoral Uncertainty
Harry He
May 26, 2026
Abstract
Why do firms sometimes contribute to electorally secure candidates and at other
times support candidates in competitive races? I argue that corporate political ac-
tion committee (PAC) contributions reflect a common investment problem under elec-
toral uncertainty. Firms weigh the expected benefits of supporting future officeholders
against the costs of contributing to candidates who may lose, producing a single-peaked
relationship between candidates’ probability of victory and corporate contribution be-
havior. I further argue that this trade-off varies systematically with sectoral partisan
alignment: firms in sectors with stronger partisan policy stakes are more willing to
tolerate electoral risk and support vulnerable candidates, while firms in weakly aligned
sectors behave more like access-oriented contributors. I test these predictions using a
novel firm–candidate panel linking Federal Election Commission corporate PAC con-
tributions to firm characteristics, Inside Elections race ratings, and market-based mea-
sures of sectoral partisan advantage across U.S. House elections from 2010 to 2022.
The results show that corporate PAC giving follows an inverse U-shaped pattern, with
contributions concentrated among candidates who are electorally viable but not cer-
tain to win. This pattern varies across firms: firms in partisan-advantaged sectors are
more likely to support challengers and electorally vulnerable candidates. The findings
show that access-oriented and election-oriented accounts of corporate political giving
are distinct strategies emerging from a shared decision problem under different firm-
and sector-level political conditions.
1 Introduction
Business interests are central participants in American electoral politics, yet we know less
about when corporate money follows political power and when it attempts to shape it. Cor-
porate PACs often support incumbents and likely winners, suggesting an access-oriented
strategy of cultivating relationships with future officeholders. But they also contribute to

competitive races and vulnerable candidates, where marginal resources may be more elec-
torally consequential. Why do firms sometimes invest in political access and at other times
invest in electoral outcomes?
Existing research has long treated corporate political giving as a form of investment, but
it offers competing expectations about how firms distribute contributions across candidates.
Access-oriented accounts theorize long-term relationships and predict that firms concentrate
resources on likely winners to secure relationships with future policymakers (Welch 1980;
Snyder 1990; Herrnson, Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020). Election-oriented accounts instead
emphasize short-term calculations and contributions to competitive races, where marginal re-
sources may influence electoral outcomes (McCarty and Rothenberg 2000; Box-Steffensmeier,
Radcliffe, and Bartels 2005). These perspectives are often presented as competing explana-
tions for corporate PAC behavior, but both patterns are commonly observed in practice.
I argue that these competing patterns reflect a common decision problem: firms allocate
political investments under electoral uncertainty. Before electoral outcomes are resolved,
firms must decide whether to contribute to candidates whose future political status remains
uncertain. Contributions made at this stage are public and largely irreversible, exposing
firms to the risk of backing losing candidates while offering the potential benefits of elec-
toral influence, early access, and loyalty signaling. 1 This trade-off implies that contribution
incentives are not monotonic in electoral probability. Firms should avoid both longshot can-
didates, where the risk of loss is high, and prohibitive favorites, where the marginal value
of additional support is low. The result is a single-peaked relationship between electoral
probability and contribution behavior.
This framework also clarifies why firms should differ in their contribution strategies. I
focus on sectoral partisan alignment : the extent to which firms in a sector are expected to
benefit more from one party’s control of government than from the other’s. When partisan

can be politically consequential. This partisan asymmetry raises the expected benefit of
investing before electoral uncertainty is resolved and increases firms’ willingness to tolerate
electoral risk. Firms in partisan-advantaged sectors should therefore be more willing to
support challengers and electorally vulnerable candidates. By contrast, firms in weakly
aligned or non-advantaged sectors should place greater weight on maintaining relationships
across parties and concentrate contributions on safer candidates. Access-buying and election-
buying should therefore be understood as distinct strategies that emerge from a common
investment problem under different firm-level political conditions.
Corporate PAC contributions are only one channel through which firms participate in
politics. Firms also devote substantial resources to lobbying, super PAC contributions,
independent expenditures, and less visible forms of political activity. Executives may also
play important roles in advancing firms’ strategic interests (Teso 2025), and firms may rely
on less visible channels such as charitable giving (Bertrand et al. 2020), especially when
direct political spending risks backlash. Nevertheless, corporate PAC contributions provide
an especially useful setting for analyzing corporate political strategy because they are direct,
public, candidate-specific, and repeatedly allocated across electoral contexts. Their visibility
may also make them a conservative setting for observing aggressive or election-oriented
strategies,(Richter and Werner 2017) implying that heterogeneity in PAC giving may only
understate heterogeneity in firms’ broader political activity.
I test these predictions in U.S. House elections from 2010 to 2022, a setting in which
firms repeatedly allocate resources across a large pool of candidates facing varying levels of
electoral uncertainty. I construct a novel firm–candidate panel linking Federal Election Com-
mission (FEC) corporate PAC contributions to sponsoring firm identifiers from LSEG Data &

vantage. The main analysis uses all corporate PAC contributions to House candidates within
each election cycle, with pre-election contributions during election years before Election Day

analyzed as a robustness check. The results show a clear inverse U-shaped relationship be-
tween candidates’ probability of victory and corporate PAC contribution behavior. Firms
concentrate contributions on candidates who are electorally viable but not certain to win.
This pattern varies systematically across firms. Firms in partisan-advantaged sectors reach

The paper makes three contributions. First, it reconciles access-buying and election-
buying accounts of corporate PAC behavior by showing that both can arise from a common
investment problem under electoral uncertainty. Second, it presents a new dataset connecting
corporate PAC contributions to sponsoring firm characteristics and electoral conditions. This
includes developing a measure of sectoral partisan alignment that captures how partisan
control changes the expected value of political outcomes for different firms. Third, it shows
that corporate political behavior is systematically heterogeneous: firms do not simply differ
in how much they give or their partisan giving preferences, but in the level of electoral risk
they are willing to accept when allocating political resources. These findings suggest that
business participation in electoral politics is more strategically differentiated than aggregate
accounts of corporate giving imply.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature
on corporate political investment and highlights the role of heterogeneity in contribution
strategies. Section 3 develops a theoretical framework of campaign contributions under elec-
toral uncertainty and derives testable hypotheses on both the overall contribution pattern
and cross-firm variation. Section 4 describes the data, measurement strategy, and empiri-
cal design. Section 5 presents the main results, first documenting the baseline relationship
between electoral probability and contributions, and then examining heterogeneity and un-
derlying mechanisms across firms. Section 6 concludes.

2 Relevant Literature
The dominant framework in the study of corporate money in politics conceptualizes cam-
paign contributions as political investments. This perspective treats corporate donations
as strategic expenditures undertaken in anticipation of political access, policy influence, or
other forms of political return (Hillman and Hitt 1999; Evertsson 2018). Under this view,
firms engage in electoral politics when the expected benefits of participation outweigh its
costs and when political engagement is expected to shape the policy environment in ways
that enhance firm value. Within this investment framework, scholars have articulated sev-
eral distinct mechanisms through which contributions may generate returns. Transactional
accounts emphasize quid pro quo exchanges in which contributions are traded for favorable
policy outcomes (Baron 1989). The signaling perspective instead highlights how contri-
butions convey policy-relevant information and signal ideological alignment to politicians
(Hayes 1981; Lohmann 1995; Schnakenberg and Turner 2021). The information account,
closely linked to the lobbying literature, stresses how contributions facilitate access to pol-
icymakers and create opportunities for firms to provide expertise to resource-constrained
policymakers (Weight 1990; Hall and Deardorff 2006). Despite their differences, these ap-
proaches share a common premise, that corporate contributors behave as forward-looking
investors whose political strategies are guided by expected returns.
Within this investment framework, however, scholars disagree about how firms allocate
contributions across candidates and races. The dominant view emphasizes access-oriented
giving. In this view, firms use contributions as a way to cultivate and maintain relationships
with current or likely future officeholders. This allows them to gain access to the legislative
process (Schroedel 1986; Hall and Wayman 1990; Grier and Munger 1993) through subse-
quent lobbying efforts (Herndon 1982; Langbein 1986; Kalla and Broockman 2016). Building
on Snyder’s (1990) theory of contributions, this perspective predicts that firms concentrate
resources on candidates with higher probabilities of victory in order to invest in relationships
with anticipated policymakers (Barber 2016; Herrnson, Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020).

Subsequent work in this tradition has documented access-seeking PACs’ preferences for in-
cumbents (Jacobson 2013; Fouirnaies and Hall 2014), candidates in safer seats (Welch 1980;
Snyder 1993), and members of relevant committees (Powell and Grimmer 2016).
A second view emphasizes ideology- or election-oriented giving. Rather than treating
contributions primarily as investments in post-election access, this perspective focuses on
contributions as attempts to affect electoral outcomes or support candidates when money
is most electorally useful. In this view, PACs operate similar to party organizations or
party-connected committees that seek to maximize the number of seats under their party’s
control by allocating significant resources to electorally vulnerable candidates and well-
positioned challengers (Jacobson 1985; Herrnson 1989; Herrnson 2009). Although most
works on interest-group campaign contributions categorize corporate and trade association
PACs as access-oriented and labor unions as a “mixed” type (for example, see Herrnson,
Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020), some suggest different ways to categorize interest-group mo-
tivations and strategies and document partisan electoral motivations among corporate donors
(Gopoian 1984; Eismeier and Pollock 1986a; Eismeier and Pollock 1986b). Without directly
referencing ideological or electoral motivations, challengers of the “long-term contract” view
of the access-buying perspective have argued that firm considerations are dominated by
short-term electoral calculations (McCarty and Rothenberg 2000; Box-Steffensmeier, Rad-
cliffe, and Bartels 2005), and recent empirical studies find that firms disproportionately sup-
port electorally vulnerable candidates, which align substantively with the election-buying
logic.
Empirical research on the effects of corporate campaign contributions has struggled to
adjudicate between these competing accounts. Studies examining the policy consequences
of corporate contributions reach divergent conclusions. Some identify tangible benefits, such
as favorable regulatory treatment (Figueiredo and Edwards 2007) or increased access to gov-
ernment contracts (Witko 2011), while others find find limited to null effects on subsequent
policy outcomes (Ansolabehere, Figueiredo, and Snyder 2003; Aggarwal 2010; Fouirnaies and

Fowler 2022). Evidence from financial markets is similarly mixed. Some studies report pos-
itive abnormal returns associated with supporting in-state candidates (Cooper, Gulen, and
Ovtchinnikov 2010) electoral winners (Huber and Kirchler 2013), whereas others detect no
systematic performance advantages (Hadani and Schuler 2013; Fowler, Garro, and Spenkuch
2020). Even when positive effects are identified, they often face identification challenges,
as it remains difficult to distinguish returns generated by political investment from broader
policy changes following electoral outcomes. As a result, empirical findings provide limited
guidance on which strategic logic best characterizes corporate behavior.
One interpretation of these mixed results is that they undermine the investment frame-
work itself. An alternative interpretation, however, is that they reflect heterogeneity in
corporate strategy. Most empirical analyses evaluate aggregate patterns of giving or aver-
age treatment effects, implicitly assuming a representative corporate donor. Studies often
contrast corporate PACs with other types of actors such as individuals, labor unions, and
party organizations, but devote less attention to systematic variations among firms them-
selves. When variations among business interests are considered, it is often embedded within
frameworks of analyzing interest-group politics more broadly (Barber and Eatough 2020) or
examined narrowly within specific sectors, such as oil (Evans 1988), automobile (Hersch
and McDougall 2000), or tobacco and alcohol (Taylor 2003). While these studies provide
valuable insights, they typically focus on sector specific patterns or within-sector dynamics
rather than developing generalizable frameworks for cross-sectoral variation. A notable ex-
ception is Gimpel et al. (2014), who document substantial variation in partisan alignment
across industries. Although corporations lean Republican in aggregate, partisan preferences
and alignment vary significantly, with many industries exhibiting weak or mixed partisan
preferences. These findings suggest that the business community is far from politically ho-
mogeneous.
This paper builds on these insights by reconceptualizing the relationship between access-
buying and election-buying as a problem of strategic heterogeneity under electoral uncer-

3 Theorizing Corporate Campaign Contributions
Building on the investment framework, I develop a theory of corporate campaign contri-
butions under electoral uncertainty. The central claim is that access-oriented and election-
oriented giving are not mutually exclusive explanations, but distinct strategies that can
emerge from a common decision problem. Before electoral outcomes are known, firms must
decide whether to commit resources when support may generate greater political returns
but also exposes them to greater electoral risk. By placing candidates’ probability of victory
at the center of this decision, the framework generates predictions about both aggregate
contribution patterns and systematic heterogeneity across firms.
Contributions made before electoral outcomes are resolved reflect a trade-off between ex-
pected political returns and electoral risk. The potential benefits are several. Contributions
may help allied candidates win office, strengthen relationships with eventual officeholders,
and signal loyalty in ways that facilitate subsequent access to policymakers. These benefits
are unlikely to increase monotonically with a candidate’s probability of victory. Contribu-
tions to longshot candidates are unlikely to generate returns because those candidates are
unlikely to hold office, while contributions to prohibitive favorites may have limited marginal
value because these candidates already expect to win and can attract many contributors.
8
--- PAGE 9 ---
The expected return to early support should therefore be highest for candidates who are
electorally viable but not yet safe, for whom contributions are both consequential and infor-
mative. The costs can also be substantial. Contributions consume limited PAC resources
and become sunk once made. They may also generate relational or reputational costs if a
firm backs a losing candidate and alienates the opposing candidate or party. Supporting
losing candidates may further reduce a PAC manager’s perceived effectiveness by lowering
the PAC’s win-loss record (Herrnson, Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020). Firms must therefore
decide not simply whether a candidate is likely to win, but whether the expected political
return to contributing outweighs the financial, relational, and reputational risks of doing so
before uncertainty is resolved.
To formalize this decision, consider a firm deciding whether to contribute to a candidate
with probability of victory p ∈ [0, 1]. Contributing before the outcome is resolved entails a
cost and yields a benefit if the candidate wins, but it incurs a penalty if the candidate loses.
The expected payoff to contributing is thus a function of three components: the benefit
of supporting a winner, the cost of contribution, and the penalty associated with backing
a loser. Details of the formal model is presented in Appendix A. Crucially, each of these
components may vary with electoral conditions. The value of early access may be greater in
competitive races, where support is more valuable to candidates, and weaker in safe races,
where politicians face less need to cultivate contributors. Similarly, candidates with stronger
electoral positions may be able to demand greater contributions in exchange for access.
Finally, the reputational or relational penalty of backing a losing candidate may depend on
how credible future retaliation is expected to be. These relationships imply that the expected
payoff to contributing is not mechanically increasing in a candidate’s probability of victory.
Two general implications follow from this model.
Proposition 1: Entry Threshold There exists a cutoff probability p∗ ∈ (0, 1) such that firms
do not contribute to candidates whose probability of victory falls below this threshold.
Intuitively, contributing to candidates with negligible chances of winning yields negative
9
--- PAGE 10 ---
expected returns, as the cost of investment cannot be offset by potential benefits. Firms
therefore avoid hopeless candidates and enter only when the probability of victory exceeds
a positive threshold.
Proposition 2: Single-Peaked Contribution Incentive Conditional on entry, the expected re-
turn to contributing is a single-peaked inverse U-shaped function of the probability of victory.
The single-peaked structure reconciles access-oriented and election-oriented accounts of
corporate giving. When the peak occurs close to electoral certainty, observed behavior
resembles access-buying. Firms concentrate resources on candidates who are likely to become
officeholders. When the peak occurs in the competitive range, observed behavior resembles
election-buying. Firms target candidates for whom marginal support may be more electorally
consequential. The difference between these patterns is therefore not whether firms are
strategic investors, but where their contribution incentive peaks as electoral risk changes.
Figure 1 illustrates this generalized contribution curve. The curve displays both an entry
threshold and an interior maximum. When the peak occurs near certainty, contribution
behavior resembles canonical access-buying accounts, such as Snyder’s (1990) model, in
which firms give more to safer candidates. When the peak lies in the competitive range,
behavior resembles election-buying accounts, including McCarty and Rothenberg (2000), in
which firms target contested races.
This single-peaked structure has direct empirical implications. At low levels of electoral
support, increases in a candidate’s probability of victory raise the likelihood of receiving
contributions, as the risk of backing a loser declines. However, as candidates approach near-
certain victory, the marginal incentive to contribute diminishes and may reverse. Empirically,
this implies a nonlinear relationship between electoral probability and contribution behavior.
The effect of p should be positive at low levels, flatten as p increases, and decline at high
levels. Linear specifications adopted frequently in existing studies impose monotonicity and
may obscure the underlying strategic trade-off.

This framework differs from existing models in two respects. First, rather than treating
access-buying and election-buying as competing accounts, it nests them within a single con-
tribution function. Existing access-oriented accounts emphasize the value of relationships
with likely officeholders, while election-oriented accounts emphasize the value of supporting
vulnerable candidates. The framework developed here shows that both patterns can arise
from the same investment logic once electoral probability affects both the expected benefits
and expected costs of contributing. Second, the framework identifies where firm heterogene-
ity should matter. Firms need not differ only in their overall propensity to give. They may
also differ in the level of electoral risk they are willing to accept. This implies that the loca-
tion of the contribution peak, not only the height of the contribution curve, is theoretically
meaningful.
Hypothesis 1: Firms are more likely to contribute to candidates with intermediate-to-high
probabilities of victory and less likely to contribute to either longshot candidates or near-
certain winners.
The same framework also generates predictions about firm heterogeneity. The location
of the contribution peak depends on how firms value the expected benefits of contributing
relative to the risks of backing losing candidates. Firms that place greater weight on downside

--- PAGE 12 ---
risk should require greater electoral certainty before contributing, shifting their peak toward
safer candidates. Firms that expect larger policy returns from a candidate’s victory should
be willing to contribute at lower probabilities of victory, shifting their peak toward more
competitive races.
I focus on sectoral partisan alignment as one source of this variation. In sectors where
policy outcomes are closely tied to partisan control, electoral outcomes have asymmetric
consequences for firm value. A victory by the sector’s favored party may create a more
favorable policy environment, while defeat may produce materially worse regulatory, tax,
procurement, or market conditions. This partisan asymmetry increases the expected return
to supporting candidates whose victories remain uncertain but consequential. Firms in
partisan-advantaged sectors should therefore tolerate greater electoral risk and shift their
contribution activity toward candidates who are viable but not yet safe. By contrast, firms
in weakly aligned or non-advantaged sectors face weaker partisan policy stakes. These firms
may still value access to policymakers, but the expected payoff from changing partisan
control is lower or more ambiguous. They therefore have stronger incentives to avoid failed
investments, maintain relationships across party lines, and concentrate contributions on
candidates with higher probabilities of victory. In the language of the model, sectoral partisan
alignment shifts the location of the contribution peak. Stronger alignment moves the peak
toward more electorally vulnerable candidates, while weaker alignment moves it toward safer
candidates.
This heterogeneity can appear through two observable channels. First, firms in partisan-
advantaged sectors may differ in candidate-type targeting. Incumbents usually offer greater
access certainty because they already hold office and are more likely to maintain relation-
ships with contributors. Access-oriented firms should therefore disproportionately favor in-
cumbents. Firms more willing to bear electoral risk, by contrast, should be relatively more
willing to support challengers, who often lack the established access value of incumbents but
may be more electorally consequential. Second, firms may differ in their response to electoral
12
--- PAGE 13 ---
probability within candidate type. Even among incumbents, challengers, or open-seat candi-
dates, firms in partisan-advantaged sectors may be more willing to support candidates with
lower probabilities of victory. Distinguishing these channels is important because both can
produce lower average contribution peaks, but they imply different mechanisms. Candidate-
type targeting operates through the kinds of candidates firms select, while within-type risk
tolerance reflects how firms evaluate electoral uncertainty among otherwise similar candi-
dates.
These two channels generate distinct observable implications. If sectoral partisan align-
ment increases firms’ tolerance for electoral risk, then firms in partisan-advantaged sectors
should reach their maximum contribution propensity at lower probabilities of victory. If
this tolerance operates partly through candidate-type targeting, then firms in partisan-
advantaged sectors should place less weight on incumbency and become relatively more
willing to support challengers and open-seat candidates.
Hypothesis 2: As sectoral partisan alignment increases, firms’ contribution peaks shift to-
ward candidates with lower probabilities of victory.
Hypothesis 3: As sectoral partisan alignment increases, firms place less weight on incum-
bency and become more willing to support challengers.
Taken together, the theory implies that observed differences between access-buying and
election-buying behavior reflect variation in firm characteristics and electoral conditions
rather than competing theoretical logics. Firms face a common decision problem under
electoral uncertainty, but differ in how they resolve the trade-off between expected bene-
fits and electoral risk. As a result, aggregate patterns of contribution behavior may mask
systematic heterogeneity in how firms allocate political resources.

4 Data and Methods
The empirical analysis constructs a novel firm–candidate panel linking corporate PAC con-
tributions to firm characteristics, electoral competitiveness, and sectoral partisan alignment.
The unit of analysis is the firm–candidate–election cycle. For each firm–candidate–cycle,
the main dependent variable indicates whether the firm’s corporate PAC contributed to the
candidate at any point during the election cycle.
Contribution data come from the Federal Election Commission (FEC), which records all
PAC donations to federal candidates. I focus my attention on House of Representatives can-
didates as all members face re-election every two years. I also restrict attention to corporate
PACs, the primary legal vehicle through which corporations contribute directly to politi-
cal campaigns. The main analysis uses all contributions recorded between corporate PACs
and House candidates within each election cycle. However, full-cycle giving may include
post-election “kiss-and-make-up” contributions to newly elected challengers and open-seat
candidates or politicians who recently received relevant committee assignments (Herrnson,
Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020; Powell and Grimmer 2016). To address this potential con-
cern, I re-estimate the main models using a pre-election sample, which includes only con-
tributions made during election years before Election Day. These results are reported as
robustness checks and show that the main findings are consistent across different sample
specifications.
Because the FEC does not provide unique firm identifiers, I hand-matched PACs to
sponsoring corporations using firm names, addresses, and organizational information from
LSEG Data & Analytics. This process reconciles mergers, acquisitions, subsidiaries, and
name changes over time. Automated matching alone produces substantial errors, particularly
for legacy PACs, and the manual procedure significantly improves the accuracy of linking
contributions to firm-level characteristics.
To measure electoral uncertainty, I use candidate ratings from Inside Elections , which
provides nonpartisan expert assessments and projections of federal races on a nine-point scale
14
--- PAGE 15 ---
ranging from “Solid Republican” to “Solid Democrat.” I convert these ratings into a measure
of each candidate’s ex ante probability of victory. This measure serves as an empirical
proxy for p, the candidate’s perceived likelihood of winning in the theoretical framework.
Unlike realized vote shares, which are observed only after uncertainty resolves, expert ratings
capture the competitive environment as firms and candidates themselves perceive it at the
time contributions are made.
Another central explanatory variable is sectoral partisan alignment, defined as the extent
to which firms in a sector are expected to benefit more from one party’s control of government
than from the other’s. The concept is not intended to measure firms’ expressed ideology
or partisan identity. Instead, it captures market-implied exposure to partisan control, or
whether investors expect firms in a sector to perform better under one partisan coalition
than under the other. I measure this concept using stock-market reactions to salient partisan
political shocks, which provide moments when expectations about partisan control change
sharply and can be observed independently of firms’ own contribution behavior. Specifically,
I use an event-study approach to estimate the abnormal returns around presidential elections
without an incumbent seeking reelection and the unexpected shift in Senate control following
Senator Jim Jeffords’ decision to caucus with Democrats in May 2001. These shocks are
useful because they change expectations or resolve uncertainty about partisan control while
occurring within narrow windows, allowing market reactions to be interpreted as changes in
expected firm value associated with partisan political outcomes. For each event, I estimate
abnormal returns using intra-day or overnight windows, consistent with the assumption that
politically relevant information is rapidly incorporated into stock prices. Abnormal returns
are first estimated at the firm level and then aggregated to the sector level.
I aggregate abnormal returns to the sector level for both empirical and substantive rea-
sons. Empirically, firm-level abnormal returns around political events are noisy and may
coincide with earnings announcements, firm-specific news, liquidity constraints, or other id-
iosyncratic shocks unrelated to politics. Given the limited number of major partisan political

--- PAGE 16 ---
shocks available for constructing this measure, such noise can disproportionately influence
firm-level estimates. Aggregation reduces the influence of these idiosyncratic movements and
recovers the common component of political exposure shared by firms in similar economic
activities. Sector-level aggregation also mitigates concerns about non-random missingness.
Abnormal returns can only be estimated for publicly traded firms, and price reactions for
thinly traded firms, particularly those with low liquidity or over-the-counter trading, may
be muted or delayed. Because private firms and low-liquidity firms are not randomly dis-
tributed across sectors, relying exclusively on firm-level estimates could introduce systematic
measurement error. Aggregating to the sector level allows the measure to capture the com-
mon political exposure shared by firms within a sector, including firms for which direct
market-based estimates are unavailable or noisy.
Substantively, sector-level aggregation corresponds to the theoretical concept of shared
exposure to partisan policy regimes rather than idiosyncratic firm ideology. Many partisan
political shocks operate through sector-wide policy domains, including taxation, regulation,
procurement, energy, health care, labor, and trade. Firms within the same sector are there-
fore likely to face similar policy risks and opportunities when partisan control changes.
Aggregating to the sector level is consistent with the notion that partisan alignment reflects
shared sectoral interests rather than purely firm-specific preferences, a pattern documented
in prior research on corporate political behavior (Evans 1988; Gimpel, Lee, and Parrott
2014).
From these estimates, I construct a market-implied measure of sectoral partisan advan-
tage. The continuous measure is rescaled to range from −1 to 1, with negative values indicat-
ing sectors expected to benefit more under Democratic control and positive values indicating
sectors expected to benefit more under Republican control. The categorical measure clas-
sifies sectors as Democratic-advantaged, Republican-advantaged, or non-advantaged. The
continuous measure captures variation in the direction and intensity of partisan exposure,
while the categorical measure provides a more interpretable comparison across sector types.
16
--- PAGE 17 ---
This measure has two advantages for the present analysis. First, because it is constructed
from financial-market reactions rather than contribution behavior or other corporate polit-
ical activities, it avoids mechanically inferring partisan alignment from the outcome it is
meant to explain. Second, because it is based on expected changes in firm value around
partisan shocks, it captures policy-relevant partisan exposure even when firms do not pub-
licly identify with a party. At the same time, the measure should be interpreted as sectoral
political exposure rather than a direct measure of corporate ideology. A sector classified as
Republican- or Democratic-advantaged is one whose expected value responds more favorably
to that party’s control, not necessarily one whose firms are ideologically committed to that
party.
The measure also has limitations. Sector-level aggregation may obscure within-sector
heterogeneity, and sectors classified as non-advantaged may include both genuinely bipartisan
sectors and sectors with limited exposure to partisan political shocks. I therefore assess the
robustness of the results using two alternative measurement strategies. First, I reconstruct
the measure at a more disaggregated industry level, which reduces the risk that broad sector
categories average over politically distinct industries. Second, I construct a contribution-
based measure of partisan alignment using historical giving patterns. This latter measure
is less useful for causal interpretation because it is derived from political behavior, but it
provides a valuable check on whether the market-based classification corresponds to revealed
patterns of partisan engagement.
After merging contribution records, electoral ratings, and firm characteristics, the result-
ing panel contains approximately seven million firm–candidate observations spanning 2010 to
2022. The primary dependent variable is a binary indicator of whether a firm contributes to
a given candidate during the election cycle. In complementary specifications, I model contri-
bution amounts to assess whether the theoretical predictions extend to the intensive margin.
All models include firm-level controls that account for political capacity and exposure, in-
cluding total PAC receipts available in the election cycle (capturing fundraising capacity),
17
--- PAGE 18 ---
public versus private ownership, foreign ownership, and whether the firm is headquartered
in the same state as the candidate. Candidate- and race-level controls include incumbency
status, party affiliation, and indicators for special elections. Year and state fixed effects
account for time-varying national conditions and state-level political environments.
The empirical strategy tests two central predictions of the theory. First, I estimate base-
line models in which firms’ contribution decisions are modeled as a function of candidates’ ex
ante probability of victory, allowing for nonlinear effects to evaluate Hypothesis 1 regarding
the single-peaked relationship. Second, to assess heterogeneity in contribution strategies, I
interact sectoral partisan-advantage with electoral probability and incumbency status. The
theory predicts that partisan alignment shifts the location of the contribution peak: firms
in aligned sectors should exhibit greater tolerance for electoral risk and are more likely to
back electoral vulnerable candidates. These models also assess whether this behavior reflects
differences in candidate-type targeting or broader variation in electoral risk tolerance.
5 Results
The empirical analysis proceeds in four steps. I first examine whether corporate PAC con-
tributions follow the single-peaked pattern predicted by the theory. I then test whether the
location of this contribution peak varies with sectoral partisan alignment. Next, I examine
whether this heterogeneity operates through candidate-type targeting, broader differences
in electoral risk tolerance, or both. Finally, I assess whether the same patterns hold for
contribution amounts and under alternative specifications.

5.3 Contribution Amounts
The preceding analyses examine whether a firm contributes to a candidate. I next exam-
ine the level of financial support allocated to each firm–candidate pair. While the baseline
analysis focuses on the extensive margin or whether a firm contributes to a candidate, the
theoretical framework can also be applied to the level of contributions. Modeling contribu-
tion amounts requires accounting for the structure of campaign finance data. Contribution
amounts are bounded below at zero because firms cannot make negative contributions, and
non-contributions are common. Federal law also imposes an upper bound on the amount
a corporate PAC may contribute to a candidate. Corporate PACs may contribute at most
$5,000 per candidate per election, implying a maximum of $10,000 over the primary and
general elections within a two-year cycle. As a result, the observed contribution amounts
are censored both from below at zero and from above at $10,000. I use a Tobit specification
that accounts for both forms of censoring.

5.4 Robustness Checks
I assess the robustness of the main findings across several alternative empirical choices, in-
cluding the timing of contributions, the functional form of electoral probability, the level of
partisan-alignment measurement, and the construction of the partisan-alignment measure.
The goal is to assess whether the hypothesized contribution pattern and cross-sectoral het-
erogeneity are sensitive to data selection, functional form choices, or the construction of key
variables.
First, I examine whether the results are sensitive to the timing of contributions. The
main analysis uses all contribution records within each election cycle, which captures the
full allocation of corporate PAC resources across House candidates. This design reflects
the fact that firms allocate political resources throughout the electoral cycle and avoids
selecting only a subset of observed giving. However, some contributions occur after electoral
uncertainty has been resolved. These post-election contributions may include “kiss-and-
make-up” giving to newly elected candidates or contributions following important committee
assignments (Herrnson, Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020; Powell and Grimmer 2016). Such
contributions are not inconsistent with access-seeking behavior, but they are less directly tied
to the theory’s emphasis on contribution decisions under uncertainty. I therefore re-estimate
the main models using only contributions made during election years before Election Day.
This restricted sample isolates contributions made before electoral outcomes are known and
provides a closer test of the timing logic emphasized by the theory. The results, reported in
Appendix B.1, are substantively similar and in some specifications stronger, indicating that
the main patterns are not driven by contributions made after electoral uncertainty has been
resolved.
Second, I examine whether the inverse-U pattern is an artifact of the quadratic specifica-
31
--- PAGE 32 ---
tion. The baseline models use a quadratic term in candidates’ probability of victory because
the theoretical framework predicts a single-peaked relationship and because the peak lo-
cation provides an interpretable quantity for comparing contribution strategies across firm
types. Nevertheless, imposing a quadratic functional form may mechanically induce curva-
ture. To address this concern, I re-estimate the models using a nonparametric cubic spline
specification with three knots. This approach allows the relationship between electoral prob-
ability and contribution behavior to be more flexibly determined by the data. The resulting
predicted relationships, shown in Appendix B.2, closely replicate the baseline findings, ex-
hibiting a broadly inverse-U pattern and similar differences in peak location across firm
types. This suggests that the core results are not driven by the imposed quadratic structure.
Third, I examine whether the findings are robust to measuring partisan alignment at a
more disaggregated level. The main analysis uses sector-level aggregation because the theo-
retical concept concerns shared exposure to partisan policy regimes and because aggregation
reduces idiosyncratic firm-level noise in abnormal returns. However, sectors may vary in
internal diversity. Some sectors are relatively homogeneous, while others contain industries
with distinct policy exposures and potentially different partisan sensitivities. Industry-level
aggregation provides a useful robustness check as it preserves the logic of aggregation while
reducing the possibility that broad sector categories average over politically distinct in-
dustries. I therefore reconstruct the partisan-alignment measure at the industry level and
re-estimate the main specifications. The results, reported in Appendix B.3, remain qualita-
tively unchanged. Firms in partisan-advantaged industries continue to exhibit contribution
peaks at lower probabilities of victory relative to firms in non-advantaged industries. This
indicates that the main findings are not driven by the particular level of aggregation used in
the baseline measure.
Finally, I examine whether the results are robust to an alternative contribution-based
measure of partisan alignment. The market-implied measure has the advantage of being
constructed independently of contribution behavior, but it cannot fully distinguish between
32
--- PAGE 33 ---
two conceptually distinct cases. Some non-advantaged sectors may be genuinely bipartisan,
maintaining relationships with policymakers from both parties, while others may appear
non-advantaged because they are simply less exposed to partisan political shocks. While
these cases are observationally similar in a market-based measure—both producing weak
or near-zero abnormal return responses, they may imply different underlying incentives for
political engagement. To address this limitation, I construct an alternative measure based
on historical contribution patterns, similar to the party proximity index developed by Amy
D. Meli, “Party People? Measuring Interest Groups’ Proximity to Political Parties,” In-
terest Groups & Advocacy 12 (2023). This measure captures revealed patterns of partisan
engagement in firms’ political giving. Because it is derived from contribution behavior, it
introduces potential endogeneity concerns and cannot be interpreted as exogenous to firms’
strategic decisions. Nevertheless, it provides a useful validation check. Re-estimating the
main models using the contribution-based measure yields results consistent with the baseline
findings, as shown in Appendix B.4. Republican-aligned firms exhibit a greater propensity to
target electorally vulnerable candidates, whereas bipartisan firms concentrate more heavily
on candidates with higher probabilities of victory.
6 Conclusion
This paper develops and tests a framework for understanding corporate PAC contributions as
political investments under electoral uncertainty. The central claim is that access-oriented
and election-oriented giving are not competing explanations, but distinct strategies that
emerge from a common decision problem. Firms weigh the expected benefits of supporting
candidates before outcomes are resolved against the financial, relational, and reputational
risks of backing losing candidates. This trade-off produces a nonmonotonic relationship
between candidates’ probability of victory and corporate PAC giving.
The empirical results support this framework. Using a novel firm–candidate panel of
33
--- PAGE 34 ---
corporate PAC contributions in U.S. House elections from 2010 to 2022, I show that firms
concentrate contributions on candidates who are electorally viable but not certain to win.
This pattern varies systematically across firms. Firms in partisan-advantaged sectors reach
their maximum contribution propensity at lower probabilities of victory, place less weight on
incumbency, and are relatively more willing to support challengers. The amount models and
robustness checks show that these patterns extend to the level of support as well and are not
driven by a particular timing window, functional form, aggregation level, or measurement
strategy.
These findings speak directly to longstanding debates about why firms give. Access-
oriented accounts emphasize contributions to likely winners, incumbents, and institutionally
powerful legislators as investments in long-term relationships with future policymakers (Sny-
der 1990; Snyder 1993; Jacobson 2013; Fouirnaies and Hall 2014; Powell and Grimmer 2016).
Election-oriented accounts emphasize short-term electoral conditions and predicts more con-
tributions to competitive races and vulnerable candidates, where marginal support may be
more electorally consequential (McCarty and Rothenberg 2000; Box-Steffensmeier, Radcliffe,
and Bartels 2005). The results here suggest that these perspectives capture different parts
of a broader strategic logic. Firms in weakly aligned or non-advantaged sectors behave more
like access-oriented contributors, concentrating support on safer candidates and incumbents.
Firms in partisan-advantaged sectors behave more like election-oriented actors, allocating
more support toward candidates whose victories remain uncertain but politically consequen-
tial. Access-buying and election-buying therefore correspond to different ways firms resolve
the same investment problem under electoral uncertainty.
The paper also contributes to research on heterogeneity among business interests. Prior
work shows that corporate PACs’ partisan preferences vary substantially across industries
(Gimpel, Lee, and Parrott 2014), and that PACs within particular sectors can adopt distinc-
tive contribution strategies (Evans 1988; Hersch and McDougall 2000; Taylor 2003). I build
on these insights by linking sectoral variation to a generalizable mechanism. Firms facing
34
--- PAGE 35 ---
stronger partisan policy stakes are willing to accept greater electoral risk when allocating
campaign contributions. Sectoral partisan alignment therefore does not merely capture which
party a sector is expected to favor; it also shapes how firms distribute resources across the
electoral-risk spectrum.
These findings also qualify the conventional view of corporate PACs as primarily access-
seeking actors. In aggregate, corporate PAC giving often appears risk-averse. Firms dispro-
portionately support incumbents, electorally viable candidates, and politicians likely to hold
office. The results here show that this characterization is incomplete. Firms in partisan-
advantaged sectors systematically behave more like election-oriented actors, directing rel-
atively more support toward challengers and competitive races. In other words, there is
money from both sides of the aisle that behave similar to party organizations or ideological
interest groups
This has important normative implications for congressional politics. If business contri-
butions are understood only as access-seeking investments in likely officeholders, corporate
PACs appear primarily as actors seeking influence after political power is established. The ev-
idence here suggests that, for some firms, corporate PAC giving also operates before political
power is resolved, in ways that resemble the strategic allocation patterns of party-affiliated
or ideological organizations. This does not imply that all corporate PACs are election-buying
actors, nor that campaign contributions are the only or most important channel of corpo-
rate political activity. But it does suggest that business participation in electoral politics
is more politically differentiated than standard access-oriented accounts imply. As policy
conflict increasingly maps onto partisan control, firms with strong partisan policy stakes
may have greater incentives to intervene in competitive congressional elections rather than
merely cultivate relationships with likely winners.
Future research can extend this framework by examining additional dimensions of firm
heterogeneity. This paper focuses on sectoral partisan alignment because partisan policy
stakes shape firms’ willingness to tolerate electoral risk. But firms may also differ in other
35

A Appendix: Access-Buying Model
I begin with the simplest case: a firm faces a single election with two candidates, D and R.
Before the election, the firm observes that candidate D is expected to win with probability
p ∈ [0, 1] and decides whether to contribute to D. Contributing requires a non-negative
cost c(p) ≥ 0. If D wins, the firm receives a non-negative political benefit B(p) ≥ 0, which
may include access, goodwill, policy influence, or the value of supporting an electorally
consequential ally. If D loses, the firm receives no benefit from D’s victory and may incur
a non-negative penalty λ(p) ≥ 0, reflecting reputational costs, relational costs, or reduced
access to the opposing candidate or party. 4
Normalizing the utility of not contributing to zero, the expected utility of contributing
to D is
E[U (p)] = p · U (B(p) − c(p)) + (1 − p) · U

The costs, benefits, and penalties associated with contributing need not remain con-
stant across electoral conditions. I impose four assumptions that capture how the value of
contributing changes with candidate D’s probability of victory.
Assumption 1: The benefit of contributing conditional on D’s victory is weakly decreasing
and concave in p: B′(p) ≤ 0 and B′′(p) ≤ 0.
This assumption captures the idea that early support is more valuable when the can-
didate is viable but electorally uncertain. In an election-oriented interpretation, marginal
support is more valuable when the candidate faces a competitive race. In an access-oriented
interpretation, support offered when victory is uncertain can generate more goodwill than
support offered after victory is nearly assured. This assumption follows from the logic that
the cost of waiting is positive for investments under uncertainty (Bernanke 1983).
Assumption 2: The cost or price of contributing is weakly increasing and convex in p:
c′(p) ≥ 0 and c′′(p) ≥ 0.
This assumption reflects the idea that candidates with stronger electoral prospects can
command greater support from contributors. Candidates who are more likely to win are
more attractive to access-seeking contributors and therefore face less need to offer access or
goodwill cheaply. This follows from the candidate-centered models of campaign contributions
(for example, see Snyder 1991).

Assumption 3: The penalty associated with backing a losing candidate is weakly decreasing

assumption because the cost of waiting to contribute is positive, and even post-election

contributing before the election is strictly positive.

will resemble election-buying: firms target candidates for whom marginal support may be
more electorally consequential. The difference between these patterns is therefore captured
by the location of the contribution peak.

Firm Heterogeneity and Sectoral Partisan Alignment.
The location of the contribution peak also depends on the firm’s expected benefits, costs,
and penalties. Let Bi(p), ci(p), and λi(p) denote these functions for firm i. Firms that expect

larger political returns from a candidate’s victory have higher Bi(p), making contribution
more attractive at lower probabilities of victory. Firms that face greater reputational, re-
lational, or political costs from backing losing candidates have higher λi(p), making them
more conservative and shifting their contribution peak toward safer candidates.
Sectoral partisan alignment can be represented as a shift in these functions. In sectors
where partisan control has larger expected consequences for firm value, the benefit of sup-
porting candidates associated with the favorable partisan coalition is higher. Formally, for a
firm in a partisan-advantaged sector, Bi(p) is larger over the range of electorally viable but
uncertain candidates. This increases the expected return to contributing before the outcome
is resolved and shifts the contribution peak toward lower values of p. By contrast, firms in
weakly aligned or non-advantaged sectors derive less additional value from changing parti-
san control and place greater weight on preserving access across parties. Their contribution
peaks should therefore occur at higher probabilities of victory.
This comparative static links the formal model to the empirical hypotheses. The baseline
model predicts a nonmonotonic relationship between candidates’ probability of victory and
contribution behavior. Heterogeneity in risk tolerance and partisan policy stakes shifts the
location of the peak. Firms in partisan-advantaged sectors should reach their maximum
contribution propensity at lower probabilities of victory, while firms in weakly aligned or
non-advantaged sectors should concentrate giving on safer candidates.

B.1 Pre-Election Sample
The main analysis uses all corporate PAC contributions to House candidates within each
election cycle. This specification captures the full allocation of corporate PAC resources
across candidates and avoids selecting only a subset of observed giving. However, some
contributions occur after electoral uncertainty has been resolved. These post-election contri-
butions may include “kiss-and-make-up” giving to newly elected challengers and open-seat
candidates (Herrnson, Panagopoulos, and Bailey 2020), as well as contributions made after
new committee assignments or other institutional developments (Powell and Grimmer 2016).
Such contributions are less directly tied to the theory’s emphasis on contribution decisions
under unresolved electoral uncertainty.
To address this, I re-estimate the main models using a restricted pre-election sample.
This sample includes contributions made during election years before Election Day 5. Ta-
ble 4 reports the logit results for the restricted sample. The estimates closely mirror the main

to imply a nonmonotonic relationship between electoral probability and contribution be-
havior. The interaction terms between electoral probability and sectoral partisan alignment
also remain negative, indicating that firms in partisan-advantaged sectors continue to place
relatively greater weight on candidates with lower probabilities of victory.

B.2 Relaxing Functional Form
The main models use a quadratic specification for candidates’ probability of victory. This
choice follows directly from the theoretical framework, which predicts a single-peaked rela-
tionship between electoral probability and contribution behavior. It also provides an inter-
pretable quantity, the location of the contribution peak, that can be compared across firm
types. Nevertheless, one concern is that the inverse-U pattern could be partly induced by
imposing a quadratic functional form.
To assess this possibility, I estimate an alternative specification using cubic splines. The
spline model relaxes the parametric restriction imposed by the quadratic specification and
allows the relationship between electoral probability and contribution propensity to be deter-

B.3 Industry Level Analysis
The main analysis measures partisan alignment at the sector level, grouping firms into 18
broad sectors. This level of aggregation is theoretically and empirically useful. Sectors cap-
ture shared exposure to broad policy regimes and reduce idiosyncratic noise in firm-level
abnormal returns. These broad categories are also easy to interpret and are frequently used
in financial discussions of how macroeconomic and political events affect different parts of
the economy. At the same time, sectors differ in size and internal diversity. Some sectors,
such as renewable energy and automobiles, are relatively narrow and homogeneous, while

B.4 Distinguishing Non-Partisan Firms
Finally, I examine an alternative way to distinguish sectors that appear weakly aligned
in the market-based measure. One limitation of the market-implied measure is that sec-
tors with abnormal returns close to zero may be substantively different from one another.
Some sectors may have low exposure to partisan political shocks and therefore exhibit little
market response when partisan control changes. Other sectors may be politically exposed
but bipartisan or hedged: firms in these sectors may need to maintain relationships with
both parties, may benefit from different parties across different policy domains, or may face
cross-pressures that prevent them from aligning strongly with either side. Both types of
sectors can appear close to zero in an event-study measure, even though they imply different
contribution strategies.
To distinguish these cases, I construct an alternative measure based on past contribution
behavior. Using sectoral giving patterns from the previous three cycles, I classify sectors into
four groups: Republican-aligned, Democratic-aligned, bipartisan, and non-exposed. Sectors
in which a majority of firms give disproportionately to Republican candidates are classified
as Republican-aligned, and sectors in which a majority of firms give disproportionately to
Democratic candidates are classified as Democratic-aligned. Sectors in which a majority
of firms split their donations relatively evenly between the two parties, with between 40%
and 60% of contributions going to each party, are classified as bipartisan. The remaining
sectors are classified as non-exposed and serve as the comparison group. Unlike the market-
based measure, this prior-giving measure is constructed from political behavior and should
therefore be interpreted as a robustness and validation exercise rather than as the preferred
measure of sectoral partisan alignment.
